## The nines table

Allowed downtime = (1 − availability) × window. Year = 8,760 h = 525,600 min; 30 days = 43,200 min; 28 days = 40,320 min.

| Availability | Per year | Per 30 days | Per 28 days | Per week | Per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | 87.6 h (3.65 d) | 7.2 h | 6.72 h | 1.68 h | 14.4 min |
| 99.5% | 43.8 h | 3.6 h | 3.36 h | 50.4 min | 7.2 min |
| 99.9% | 8.76 h | 43.2 min | 40.3 min | 10.1 min | 86.4 s |
| 99.95% | 4.38 h | 21.6 min | 20.2 min | 5.04 min | 43.2 s |
| 99.99% | 52.6 min | 4.32 min | 4.03 min | 60.5 s | 8.64 s |
| 99.999% | 5.26 min | 25.9 s | 24.2 s | 6.05 s | 0.86 s |

Each extra nine is 10× less downtime and usually far more than 10× the effort. At five nines a month allows less time than it takes a human to read a page, so recovery must be automatic.

## Single component: MTBF and MTTR

**A = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)**

| Server failing once a month (MTBF ≈ 720 h) | Arithmetic | Availability | Downtime per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human replaces it in 1 h | 720 ÷ 721 | 99.86% | ≈ 12 h |
| Automation replaces it in 5 min | 720 ÷ 720.083 | 99.988% | ≈ 1 h |

Same hardware, about a nine better: most availability engineering is **MTTR engineering** (detect fast, fail over automatically).

## Series and parallel

| Shape | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Series (all must work) | A = A₁ × A₂ × … × Aₙ | 10 hops at 99.9%: 0.999¹⁰ ≈ **99.0%** |
| Parallel (any one works) | A = 1 − (1 − A₁)(1 − A₂)…(1 − Aₙ) | 2 copies at 99%: 1 − 0.01² = **99.99%** |
| Parallel inside a shared zone | (1 − (1 − Aᵢ)²) × A_zone | Two 99.5% servers in one 99.9% AZ: ≈ **99.898%**, capped by the zone |
| Parallel across zones | 1 − (1 − Aᵢ × A_zone)² | Same servers in two AZs: 1 − (1 − 0.995 × 0.999)² ≈ **99.996%** |

**Worked checkout path** (LB 99.99%, app 99.95%, DB 99.9%):

1. All single, in series: 0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.999 = **99.840%** ≈ 14.0 h/yr.
2. DB standby, 6 failovers × 90 s = 9 min/yr → DB ≈ 99.998%; chain ≈ **99.938%** ≈ 5.4 h/yr.
3. Second app server: 1 − 0.0005² ≈ 99.99998%; chain ≈ **99.988%** ≈ 62 min/yr, now dominated by the load balancer.

Redundancy pays only at the weakest link; the next link then becomes the target.

<Callout type="warning" title="Where the parallel formula lies">
It assumes independent failures. Shared racks, zones, builds, config pushes, DNS, auth services and the failover machinery itself all correlate failures. A survivor that cannot carry the whole load fails next. Treat the parallel result as a best case.
</Callout>

## Dependencies and the extra nine

Your availability can never exceed the product of your **hard** dependencies. Treynor and co-authors ("The Calculus of Service Availability", ACM Queue, 2017) suggest critical dependencies offer roughly one more nine than the service: a 99.99% service with 5 hard dependencies at 99.999% spends 1 − 0.99999⁵ ≈ 0.005%, **half** its 0.01% budget, on them alone. Ways out: make the dependency soft (cache, fallback, async), or add redundancy.

## Capacity to survive a zone loss

Peak 30,000 req/s. Each surviving zone must carry peak ÷ (zones − 1).

| Zones | Per zone, normal | Per zone after one is lost | Total provisioned |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 15,000 | 30,000 | 60,000 (2× peak) |
| 3 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 45,000 (1.5× peak) |
| 4 | 7,500 | 10,000 | 40,000 (1.33× peak) |

## Error budgets (Google SRE book, ch. 3; SRE workbook, ch. 2 and 5)

- **Error budget = 1 − SLO** over the SLO window. 99.9% → 0.1% of valid events may be bad.
- **Request-based example:** 1,000 req/s × 86,400 s × 30 d = 2,592,000,000 requests; budget = **2,592,000** bad requests. A 20-minute incident at 30% errors = 1,000 × 1,200 × 0.3 = 360,000 bad = **13.9%** of the month.
- **Same thing in time:** 20 min × 30% = 6 min of full outage; 6 ÷ 43.2 = 13.9%.
- **Rolling 28-day windows** are what the workbook recommends (equal weekdays, match user memory); calendar months suit SLAs and reports.

| Term | Formula | At a 99.9% SLO, 30-day window |
|---|---|---|
| Burn rate | observed error rate ÷ (1 − SLO) | 0.1% errors → 1; 1.44% → 14.4; 30% → 300 |
| Time to exhaust | window ÷ burn rate | Burn 300 → 720 h ÷ 300 = 2.4 h |
| Budget consumed | burn rate × duration ÷ window | Burn 14.4 for 1 h → 14.4 ÷ 720 = 2% |

## Multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts (SRE workbook, "Alerting on SLOs")

| Severity | Long window | Short window | Burn rate | Budget spent when it fires |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Page | 1 h | 5 min | 14.4 | 2% |
| Page | 6 h | 30 min | 6 | 5% |
| Ticket | 3 days | 6 h | 1 | 10% |

Both windows must exceed the threshold to fire; the short window makes the alert reset quickly once the problem stops.

## Error budget policy (workbook example, paraphrased)

| Trigger | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Budget exceeded over the trailing four weeks | Halt changes except urgent fixes and security patches until back within SLO |
| One incident uses more than 20% of the four-week budget | Postmortem with at least one high-priority action |
| One class of outage uses more than 20% in a quarter | It goes on next quarter's planning priorities |
| Disagreement about the numbers or the policy | Escalate to a named executive |

The field disagrees on hard freezes (clear, but batches risk and punishes teams for dependencies' outages); many teams use softer policies. Both sides agree the consequence is written down before it is needed.

| Layer | Set by | Example | When missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| SLI | Engineering | Ratio of non-5xx responses at the load balancer | Nothing: it is a measurement |
| SLO | Engineering with product | 99.9% over 28 days | Error budget policy applies |
| SLA | Business and legal | 99.5% per calendar month | Credits or refunds |

The SLA is looser than the SLO so the team acts before money is owed.
